# Methodology

## Governing proposition

The photograph is strongest when six factors reinforce one another:

personal urgency x clear theme x subject individuality x relationship and distance x symbolic framing x selection and presentation.

If personal urgency is absent, the image follows fashion. If the theme is absent, it becomes a collection of attractive surfaces. If individuality is absent, the subject is interchangeable. If relationship is absent, the camera feels neutral, remote, or merely consuming. If symbolism is absent, the frame says little beyond the event. If selection is absent, the work depends on accident.

## Begin with four questions

Complete these before choosing camera settings:

- WHO: Who is this particular person, beyond a generic demographic label?
- WHERE: Why is this place connected to the person's life, role, memory, or present action?
- HOW: Who is holding the camera in relational terms?
- WHY NOW: What state is changing, colliding, loosening, beginning, or ending?

A useful theme sentence has this shape:

See [person with a social role and individual anchors] in [a necessary place], from [a relational position], while [state A] changes toward [state B].

The person, place, camera role, and change should not be freely interchangeable.

## Interest and restraint are separate controls

Treat the photographer's emotional interest and formal restraint as independent axes.

- High interest, high restraint: subjective selection expressed through a clear, controlled frame.
- High interest, low restraint: the chosen detail overwhelms context and relationship.
- Low interest, high restraint: precise but typological or catalog-like.
- Low interest, low restraint: a generic image with no point of view.

Default toward visible interest with stronger formal restraint. Do not confuse restraint with emotional coldness.

Suggested internal controls:

    gaze:
      emotional_charge: 65
      formal_restraint: 80

## Discover the subject additively

Do not begin from an ideal and subtract deviations. Add the details that make this person memorable:

- a small facial asymmetry
- a gesture repeated without thinking
- a preferred way of presenting themself
- an object changed by long use
- an expression that lingers after an emotion

Choose three anchors that can be seen, not merely described in biography. Keep natural texture and irregularity when they contribute to recognition.

## SIR

Build tension across three axes:

- S, Symbolicity: role, clothing, place, time, routine, or social position.
- I, Individuality: distinctive expression, habit, personal object, use mark, or irregularity.
- R, Relationship: viewer role, distance, gaze, awareness, comfort, hesitation, or equality.

One image should make at least two axes legible. A series should contain all three. Symbolicity alone becomes a type; individuality alone becomes a character study without context; relationship alone becomes an unexplained private snapshot.

## The subject's self-presentation matters

Hold two intentions at once:

- how the subject would choose to be seen
- what the photographic theme needs to reveal

Start from a subject-led presentation, then find a shared frame, then use more elliptical framing only when the relationship and context support it. The subject is a participant in the visual proposition rather than raw material.

## Details compress meaning

A detail frame is not valuable because it is close. It is valuable when one part compresses action, time, role, or relationship:

- a hand loosening an identification lanyard
- fingers holding the folded corner of a repeatedly read book
- a shoe crossing a familiar threshold
- a sleeve caught between two fingers before an answer

Begin at the meaning core and widen only as needed. An omitted face must be replaced by readable action, object, distance, clothing, or space.

## Distance is psychological

Camera distance implies a relationship:

- breath-close: strong trust and subjectivity; use only when the meaning requires it.
- conversation distance: mutual presence and a balance of person and place.
- across the room: quiet observation, solitude, or a boundary.
- far away: memory, social type, or anonymity within an environment.

Name the viewer role before the focal length. Eye level tends toward equality; a side view can remain beside the subject without claiming their interior; distance can protect ambiguity.

## Prefer transitions over poses

Design the ten seconds around the shutter:

- what just happened
- what is happening now
- what will probably happen next
- what remains unspoken
- how aware the subject is of the camera

The instant before an action settles, while it is incomplete, or just after emotion passes usually carries more time and relationship than a completed pose.

## Leave one controlled question

Do not explain every fact. Leave one question for the viewer: what was said, whether the subject will leave, why an object matters, or how close the camera holder is. Ambiguity must be structured by visible evidence, not produced by missing scene logic.
