L20: ##### What Is Palm Muting?
L21: 
L22: Muting is actually the process of keeping a ringing 【7†string†shop.fender.com】 from vibrating. When you put your palm on the strings at the point where they connect to the 【8†bridge†shop.fender.com】, you silence the vibration a little – you mute it. This is why it’s called palm muting.
L23: 
L24: Here are three keys to getting that palm mute just right:
L25: ##### 1. The Sweet Spot
L26: 
L27: You can change how muted the strings are. The more you move to the right, the less you mute and the more the strings ring out. Move to the left and you’ll mute more. There is a spot in the middle where the note will sound chunky but not dead. This is the sweet spot.
L28: 
L29: ##### 2. Firmness of the Plant
L30: 
L31: Your palm needs to be planted firmly enough for the mute to even happen. Try pressing your palm against the strings with different levels of firmness.
L32: ##### 3. Volume Adjustments
L33: 
L34: You can adjust the volume of your sound by strumming harder or more lightly as you mute. You need to balance the palm pressing against the strings with the strumming to get the sound you are looking for.
L35: Each of these three elements impact your effectiveness at palm muting. By trying different combinations and adjusting based on what you hear, you’ll eventually get what you need for the song you’re playing. Palm muting is a really dynamic technique that can make the simplest playing sound professional.

L53: ## Teach your students how to master matched and traditional grips to become a well-rounded percussionist and musician.

L66: #### UNDERSTANDING FULCRUM
L67: 
L68: Before exploring our grip options, we must understand the term fulcrum, which will be used in all grip explanations. The Oxford Dictionary defines fulcrum as “the point at which a lever rests or is supported and on which it pivots.” The lever is the drumstick, and in most circumstances, it rests between the pad of the thumb and the first or second joint of the index finger and pivots back and forth using a combination of hinges — fingers, wrist and arm.
L69: 【24†Image: proximity to instrument】
L70: #### MATCHED GRIP
L71: 
L72: This is the most common grip used for today’s percussive world of instruments. Often referred to as “the grip that keeps on giving,” matched grip is popular for its ability to be transferred across most percussive mediums without much need for change. It’s called matched grip because of the identical hold used by the left and right hands. Simply grab the stick naturally as if you are picking up a small object off a desk or table.
L73: Most percussion students start their musical journey by playing matched grip, often on practice pads or practice keyboard instruments. Matched grip can also be used when performing concert snare drum, concert toms, timpani, drum set, crash and suspended cymbals, and all major keyboard instruments, lending to its applicability across most areas of percussion playing.
L74: 
L75: To achieve the correct matched grip, address these seven checkpoints.
L76:   1. Proximity to instrument: Ensure that you are positioned comfortably to be able to play the instrument in the proper beating zones. Be careful not to stand or sit too far or too close to the instrument.
L77:   2. Front fulcrum: The stick should be held firmly by the pad of the thumb and the second joint of the index finger, about a third of the way up the stick.
L78:   3. Back fulcrum: The back three fingers (middle, ring and pinky) should wrap around the stick naturally and completely.
L79:   4. Stick direction: Underneath the hand, the stick should cross the hand diagonally with the butt end of the stick traveling across the “meaty” part of the palm
L80:   5. Wrist orientation: When in set position on the drum, keyboard or cymbal, the backs of the wrists should be almost completely flat, facing the ceiling
L81:   6. Stick angle: When playing on a single drum, the angle of the sticks should create a 90° angle. When performing on multiple drums or large intervals on a keyboard instrument, the angle of the sticks can be flexible to accommodate for the space. Stick and mallet angles should almost never go past parallel.
L82:   7. Bead placement: In most cases, beads or mallet heads should be placed in the center of the drum or keyboard bar. Special considerations should be made for cymbal and timpani playing.
L83: When working on your matched grip, avoid the following common pitfalls.
L84: 
L85: 【25†Image: incorrect grip】
L86: 
L87:   * Gap between thumb and hand: A firm fulcrum is one that does not leave a large gap between the thumb and the side of the hand. Ensure that the thumb is resting flat on the stick and close the hand around the stick to eliminate this common problem.
L88: 【26†Image: stick directionality】
L89:   * Stick directionality: Oftentimes, young players will keep the butt end of the stick either in the webbing of their fingers, or without any back finger contact at all. Ensure that the butt end of the stick is traveling diagonally across the palm, crossing the meaty part of the underside of the hand.
L90: 【27†Image: choked up too high】
L91:   * Choked up too far: When looking straight down at the stick when your wrists are flat, there should only be about one inch coming out of the back of your hand. If you are looking on the underside of your hand, two to three inches is appropriate.
L92: 【28†Image: hanging index finger】
L93: 
L94:   * Hanging index finger: Ensure that the index finger is contacting the stick on the second joint and is not pointing down.

L50: # Seven Ways to Ensure a Smooth Sound Check
L51: ## Great shows start with an efficient sound check.
L52: 
L53: There’s a reason why experienced musicians — even seasoned pros — make the effort to do a sound check before every show. Sound check is the best time to test your gear, dial in levels, settle into new surroundings, and maybe even rehearse a new song.
L54: 
L55: Here are seven things every live sound engineer can do to make sure that sound check goes smoothly. Trust me, it’s worth it: The result will almost always be a better gig.
L56: #### 1. Create an Input List
L57: One of the easiest and most effective things you can do to prep for a sound check is to create an input list — something that’s easily done with any spreadsheet application. This is a list of all sources on the stage that need to be connected to the PA system, showing the name of the instrument, the mixer channel number, and whether the source is a microphone or a 【21†DI】 (Direct Inject) box.
L58: You can also add a column that indicates the general location of the source and a section that shows onstage monitor requirements. In situations where there’s a house engineer who isn’t familiar with your band, an input list will help them understand your PA requirements.
L59: An input list can easily be created using a spreadsheet.
L60: #### 2. Design a Stage Plot
L61: A stage plot is a drawing that shows the physical layout of the instruments on stage — invaluable in helping an engineer understand where everything is located. In addition to the instruments, you should identify the microphones and DIs using numbers that match the input list, as well as locations for the monitors. It’s also helpful to label each position with the band member’s name because this will help anyone consulting the stage plot to become familiar with your group.
L62: There are software apps that can be used to create slick professional-looking stage plots, but even a hand-drawn sketch can work just fine.
L63: A stage plot shows the location of everything on stage.
L64: 
L65: By the way, you may hear the terms “downstage” and “upstage” used when talking about instrument, mic and monitor locations. These are holdovers from years ago, when theater stages were raked (sloped) toward the audience — something that’s less common today. The downstage edge is closest to the audience, while the upstage edge is at the back of the stage, typically where the drummer sits.
L66: #### 3. Use a Talkback Microphone
L67: 
L68: Communication between an engineer and the performers is vital, and a talkback microphone enables an engineer at front of house (FOH) to easily speak to the musicians on stage.
L69: 
L70: Many mixing consoles, including all models in the Yamaha 【22†MGP Series†usa.yamaha.com】, offer a dedicated talkback mic input that can be routed into the 【23†aux sends】, allowing musicians to hear the engineer through their in-ear or stage monitors.
L71: The talkback input (top left) on the Yamaha MGP16X.
L72: 
L73: Having the talkback mic ready to go before the performers even start to test their instruments will enable you to ask each musician to play their instrument while you set input levels. Be sure to 【24†set input gain properly】 and leave a little bit of headroom in case some of the musicians play louder during the show than they played at sound check (that never happens!).
L74: #### 4. Create Scenes
L75: 
L76: Some digital mixers, including Yamaha 【25†Rivage PM†usa.yamaha.com】 and 【26†TF†usa.yamaha.com】 Series models, feature a built-in library that can store and recall 【27†scenes】 containing every mixer setting. Creating a scene at a rehearsal saves time at sound check because you won’t have to start from scratch. Tip: Store each scene into a memory location, lock it, then save it again into a new location so that the original can’t accidentally be changed.
L77: 
L78: The Yamaha TF1 scene library.
L79: Some digital mixers (including all Yamaha models) can interface with offline editors that can be used to create, store and recall scenes using a computer or smart device, so even if you don’t have access to the console before sound check you can still build a scene. Creating a basic scene that includes channel names, rough aux send levels, DCA (【28†Digitally Controlled Amplifier】) assignments and onboard 【29†effects processor】 settings saves time, even if you can’t set the input gain for each channel.
L80: For example, if you know that you’ll need a compressor and a gate on the kick and snare channels, you can do the “grunt work” of naming the channels and turning on the gates and compressors in the offline editor. The file from the offline editor can be saved to a thumb drive, and loaded from the thumb drive into the mixer on-site.
